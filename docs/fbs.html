1. How the bootstrap corpus is assembled

A CLEAN CUT at 2026-07-28, never a blend: GKG + wayback before it, the daily websearch pull only after it — the forward test this leads to runs on websearch alone, and blending would make the bootstrap richer than the thing it predicts. The websearch half is itself a union of two engines: Tavily fetched 80% of it and is the only one that reaches the Dow Jones desks, Anthropic the rest, and they overlap on 0.9% — all but disjoint, so a silently dead engine is a hole, not a rounding error. Engine is inferred from the query tags, not recorded (bootstrap_corpus.engine_of).

Log y-axis — the only way 9 and 15 are visible beside 9,269. One bar per source, so every height is that source's own count.

2. Coverage over time

The same corpus at three resolutions: per month, per ISO week, and per day. Month shows whether any stretch of calendar is under-served, week is the scout's actual cadence, and day exposes the individual holes the coarser views average away. Bars are stacked by which path fetched the article — blue is GKG + wayback (97.7/day) before the dashed handoff, orange Tavily and purple Anthropic (83.1/day) after it. Anthropic searches a week back rather than a day, so the shaded tail has not finished filling and is not a gap. Flat and gap-free is the goal.

3. Articles by day of week

Where the volume sits across the week. A weekend trough is normal market-news behaviour, not a retrieval fault — this panel exists so a real gap is distinguishable from the ordinary weekly cycle.

4. Articles per beat

A beat is one standing weekly search; all 46 live in retrieval_config.json, each with a plain-English query (used verbatim on the forward path) and keyword atoms (matched against headline and URL here). Sectors and superlatives are just coverage beats, not separate files.

Orange: the 10 early-framing beats — coverage while a name is still under-the-radar, what this strategy bets on. Blue: the 36 sector-coverage beats; one superlative beat among them is the corpus's largest single source. Three early-framing beats sit at the bottom, contributing nothing.

Outlet preferences live in investor_profile.backtest.md, sources in news_sources.md. The English-origin gate (plot 1) is still in code — a wart.

5. Articles per source

The 50 largest outlets, plus everything else folded into one grey bar. A sweep dominated by a handful of publishers inherits their editorial priorities; a long tail means genuine breadth.

6. Specialty-desk reach

The outlets hand-picked in investor_profile.backtest.md as specialty_allow — the desks expected to carry the early call. On the forward path a whole search pass is restricted to them; BigQuery cannot do that, so here they are simply measured. Desks with zero articles are shown, not omitted — that is the finding.

7. News replication

The same story often runs on many sites at once; ingest merges those copies and counts how many outlets ran each one (plot 1 calls this step syndication). Most sit at 1 — a story only one outlet carried is one the market probably has not priced yet. Widest this year: 122 outlets. Log axis.

8. Articles per author

The 25 most frequent named writers, with the no-byline bucket shown in grey for scale. Wire copy, PR releases and the publisher's own name are excluded by design, so a byline here is a real person. Most of the corpus has none — that is normal for market news, not a fault. Log axis.

9. Articles per bundle

Articles are bundled before the scout reads them, so a ticker’s move-signal and its driver arrive together. The 100 largest bundles are named; the grey bar at the bottom is every other bundle combined — 5,318 of them holding 11,793 articles, of which 3,468 hold a single article and can corroborate nothing. Blue is a COMPANY bundle, every article about one firm. Green is a BEAT bundle, the fallback for articles naming no usable company: grouped by the standing search that found them, so they get a topical home instead of being read alone (68 beats, 2,397 articles). A green bar is a corpus total, not one scout call. Beat bundles are DATE-SLICED per curation because a beat is a theme rather than one story, so unlike a company bundle it may be split; the ×N on a label is how many slices that beat typically becomes in one window (median slice ~20 articles). Blue bars are also corpus totals — a company bundle is filtered to the curation window before it is read, and is never split.

10. Text provenance over time

Where each week’s article text came from. Archived is a Wayback copy taken near publication, live page is today’s version which may have been edited since, and search snippet is what the engine returned when we pulled it. At the handoff the archived and live bands go to zero and search takes over the whole bar — a clean swap, not a gap. The search band is split by engine because Tavily and Anthropic barely overlap, so each is worth paying for.

11. Article length over time

Each day’s median article length in characters; the band is the middle half. The flat 300 stretch is our own ingest cap rather than shorter news — a truncated day is one where the band collapses onto the line. The GKG era runs at a median of 157 characters, and after we removed that cap on 2026-08-24 the websearch era runs at 900. Headline-only articles are left out, since counting their zero would describe neither population.

12. Org tagging, per day

Websearch articles arrive with no company tags, so we add them each morning after the pull. Green is an article tagged with at least one company, grey is one the tagger read and correctly found no company in (a macro or roundup story), red is one it never saw. Red is the only bad colour — it means a morning the tagging did not run.

13. Bundle memberships per article

How many company bundles the average article joins — the number that decides whether bundling works at all. An article that joins none is read alone with no corroboration. The dashed line is what GKG gives the backtest, so the websearch era is healthy when the solid line sits near or above it.

14. Bundle sizes: websearch vs backtest

Bundles holding one article, two, three and so on, as a share of all bundles in each era. This is the shape check: if the two eras have the same profile then the curator reads the websearch corpus the same way it reads the backtest one. A websearch bar taller on the left means bundles are too thin.

15. Daily pull health

The operational card: is the morning cron actually firing? Each bar is one morning's pull over the trailing three weeks. Red is a cron that did not fire or collected nothing; amber is a pull that ran but under-collected. There are TWO dotted medians because there are two kinds of day: weekday 96 and weekend 43 articles, and each bar is judged against half of its OWN. A single median across both sits at the weekday level, so half of it still lands above a normal Saturday and every weekend drew amber — six of them in a trailing three weeks, which teaches the reader to ignore the one colour that is supposed to mean something. This is the one panel keyed to COLLECTION date — every other panel here buckets by publication date, which is the wrong axis for this question: a morning the cron missed still fills its publication bucket from later pulls (Anthropic searches a week back), so it reads as a thin day rather than a dead one.

Last pull: 2026-09-01. Mornings the cron fired since the handoff: 36 of 36. None missed. Every pull that ran collected something. A silent gap is the failure mode this panel exists to catch — the corpus stops growing and every downstream number keeps looking reasonable.

16. Which publishers the forward half reads

Only 74 hostnames appear in both eras, and just 39.7% of post-handoff articles come from a publisher GKG also carried. Amber bars are the biggest websearch sources GKG NEVER had — Reuters, WSJ, Barron’s and the rest of the mainstream desks; blue bars are the ones both eras share, with the GKG count beside them.

This confounds any forward-vs-backtest comparison. A difference in results between the two halves may be the method or may simply be a different set of publishers. Note also that 57% of websearch sources carry a www. prefix and none of GKG’s do; the counts here are normalised, but the RAW strings are not, which splits a publisher into two rows on panel 5. It is display-only — specialty_allow and mill_block match by substring and hit identically either way.

17. Which beats survive the handoff

43 of the 43 pre-handoff beats still fire after it, and 214 more appear that GKG never used — the websearch gather runs a wider standing-query vocabulary. So the beat set EXPANDED rather than rotated, which is the reassuring reading.

No beat stopped at the handoff — everything GKG reached is still covered.
