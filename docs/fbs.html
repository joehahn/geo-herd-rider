1. How the bootstrap corpus is assembled

A CLEAN CUT at 2026-07-28, not a blend: GKG + the wayback lede backfill before it, the daily websearch pull ONLY after it. GKG is not used past the handoff even though it has coverage there, because the forward test this leads to runs on websearch alone — blending would make the bootstrap richer than the thing it predicts. The seam is nearly invisible by volume (~101/day before, ~97/day after), but the eras are otherwise 97.7% disjoint by URL, so treat any metric that moves at the handoff as a corpus change first and a signal second. Defined in src/bootstrap_corpus.py.

2. Coverage over time

The same corpus at three resolutions: per month, per ISO week, and per day. Month shows whether any stretch of calendar is under-served; week is the scout's actual cadence, so a thin week is a thin decision; day exposes individual holes the coarser views average away. Flat and gap-free is the goal.

3. Articles by day of week

Where the volume sits across the week. A weekend trough is normal market-news behaviour, not a retrieval fault — this panel exists so a real gap is distinguishable from the ordinary weekly cycle.

4. Body text vs time

Fraction of articles containing text rather than just a bare headline. Older articles score worse because their links have rotted.

5. Lede provenance by month

Counts of where each month's text came from. Clean is the Wayback copy pulled from archive.org as written when crawled soon after publication. Biased is the current text (pulled via the GKG-provided url) which may have been edited since. None is a bare headline.

6. Wayback hit rate (of URLs attempted)

A Wayback pass fills the articles that reached the curator as a bare headline — dead links, 5xx, paywalls. Grey is the remaining hole per month, green is text recovered. The hole concentrates in the oldest months, where links have rotted, and in the NEWEST, where archive.org has not crawled yet. This panel reads the backfill's cache directly rather than the corpus file, so it moves on every rebuild instead of waiting for the multi-day pass to finish.

Mind the denominator. The headline percentage here is hits ÷ URLs ATTEMPTED — only the articles that lacked text and were queued for Wayback. It is NOT the share of the corpus carrying text, which is the stat tile above and panel 5. The two are easily confused because they currently sit a tenth of a point apart by coincidence: the hit rate is 63.3% of attempted URLs, while live-page text happens to be 63.4% of the corpus. Corpus text coverage is 87% (23% archived + 63% live).

7. Live vs archived text divergence

Most of this corpus was read from the article's page as it looks today, because that is ~2,600× faster than pulling an archived copy. The risk: today's page may no longer be what ran on the decision date.

A month-stratified sample was fetched BOTH ways — today's page and the archive.org snapshot from on-or-before that date. This plots the share where the two differ materially: a spot check on the ~87% we could not afford to fetch cleanly. Flat and low means today's page is a faithful stand-in at every age.

8. Why text is missing

The recorded reason each headline-only article failed, straight from the fetch — all code, no AI. url_recycled is a real test: the page loaded and produced text, but shared too few words with the stored headline, so that URL now serves a different story. removed is HTTP 404/410. blocked_or_paywalled is 401/403 — a paywall and a bot-wall are indistinguishable to an anonymous client, so they share one honest label. no_text_on_page is a 200 with no prose; audited separately, 82% are genuine walls and 18% our parser failing.

9. Articles per beat

A beat is one standing weekly search; all 46 live in retrieval_config.json, each with a plain-English query (used verbatim on the forward path) and keyword atoms (matched against headline and URL here). Sectors and superlatives are just coverage beats, not separate files.

Orange: the 10 early-framing beats — coverage while a name is still under-the-radar, what this strategy bets on. Blue: the 36 sector-coverage beats; one superlative beat among them is the corpus's largest single source. Three early-framing beats sit at the bottom, contributing nothing.

Outlet preferences live in investor_profile.backtest.md, sources in news_sources.md. The English-origin gate (plot 1) is still in code — a wart.

10. Articles per source

The 50 largest outlets, plus everything else folded into one grey bar. A sweep dominated by a handful of publishers inherits their editorial priorities; a long tail means genuine breadth.

11. Specialty-desk reach

The outlets hand-picked in investor_profile.backtest.md as specialty_allow — the desks expected to carry the early call. On the forward path a whole search pass is restricted to them; BigQuery cannot do that, so here they are simply measured. Desks with zero articles are shown, not omitted — that is the finding.

12. News replication

The same story often runs on many sites at once; ingest merges those copies and counts how many outlets ran each one (plot 1 calls this step syndication). Most sit at 1 — a story only one outlet carried is one the market probably has not priced yet. Widest this year: 122 outlets. Log axis.

13. Articles per author

The 25 most frequent named writers, with the no-byline bucket shown in grey for scale. Wire copy, PR releases and the publisher's own name are excluded by design, so a byline here is a real person. Most of the corpus has none — that is normal for market news, not a fault. Log axis.

14. Articles per bundle

Articles are bundled before the scout reads them, so a ticker’s move-signal and its driver arrive together. The 100 largest bundles are named; the grey bar at the bottom is every other bundle combined — 19 of them holding 19 articles, of which 19 hold a single article and can corroborate nothing. Blue is a COMPANY bundle, every article about one firm. Green is a BEAT bundle, the fallback for articles naming no usable company: grouped by the standing search that found them, so they get a topical home instead of being read alone (119 beats, 11,413 articles). A green bar is a corpus total, not one scout call. Beat bundles are DATE-SLICED per curation because a beat is a theme rather than one story, so unlike a company bundle it may be split; the ×N on a label is how many slices that beat typically becomes in one window (median slice ~20 articles). Blue bars are also corpus totals — a company bundle is filtered to the curation window before it is read, and is never split.

15. The handoff seam

The bootstrap is deliberately TWO regimes with a clean cut at 2026-07-28, not a blend: GKG with the wayback backfill before it, the daily websearch pull after. Every other panel on this page aggregates the two, which is exactly wrong for judging forward readiness — only the post-handoff half resembles production.

Blue is GKG (100.8/day), amber is websearch (85.8/day). The axis is CALENDAR-COMPLETE, so a day the pull missed draws as a hole rather than being skipped. Known confound: the run-up across 07-25..07-29 (15 → 33 → 48 → 85 → 102) is unexplained, plausibly a Tavily quota or config change, so any before/after comparison spanning that date is not a retrieval result.

16. What the curator actually reads, per era

The sharpest difference on this page, and it is not visible anywhere else. Post-handoff articles carry no lede at all — 0% archived, 0% live — so the scout sees only the search snippet. Pre-handoff articles get a wayback lede written into the same field.

Median characters the scout is handed: 83 before the handoff, 300 after. The forward test therefore reads a DIFFERENT amount of text per article than the backtest that validated it — in this corpus more, because a Tavily snippet is longer than a raw GKG one, but capped and never enriched. If the wayback backfill is what makes the backtest work, the forward era does not have it.

17. Daily pull health

The operational card: is the morning cron actually firing? Bars are the trailing three weeks; the dashed line is the post-handoff median. Last day in the corpus: 2026-08-21. Zero-article days since the handoff: 0. Days absent entirely: 0. Two days were already lost on 2026-07-11/12 when cron did not fire, and a silent gap is the failure mode this panel exists to catch — the corpus simply stops growing and every downstream number keeps looking reasonable.

18. Which publishers the forward half reads

Only 58 hostnames appear in both eras, and just 41.0% of post-handoff articles come from a publisher GKG also carried. Amber bars are the biggest websearch sources GKG NEVER had — Reuters, WSJ, Barron’s and the rest of the mainstream desks; blue bars are the ones both eras share, with the GKG count beside them.

This confounds any forward-vs-backtest comparison. A difference in results between the two halves may be the method or may simply be a different set of publishers. Note also that 58% of websearch sources carry a www. prefix and none of GKG’s do; the counts here are normalised, but the RAW strings are not, which splits a publisher into two rows on panel 10. It is display-only — specialty_allow and mill_block match by substring and hit identically either way.

19. Which beats survive the handoff

42 of the 43 pre-handoff beats still fire after it, and 106 more appear that GKG never used — the websearch gather runs a wider standing-query vocabulary. So the beat set EXPANDED rather than rotated, which is the reassuring reading.

1 beat(s) stopped entirely: upcoming FDA decision clinical readout stock anticipation (125 pre-articles). A beat that produced articles before the handoff and none after is a forward BLIND SPOT — the thesis it was written to catch is no longer being looked for.
